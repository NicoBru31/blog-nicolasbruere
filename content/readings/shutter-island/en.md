## Summary

U.S. Marshal Teddy Daniels and his partner Chuck investigate on Shutter Island, a prison and asylum off the coast of Boston, in the 1950s. They have come to find a prisoner, Rachel Solando, who mysteriously escaped from her cell. The elements are against them: a storm of rare violence is raging and will cut them off from the world for several days, and the asylum staff don't seem willing to help either. Worse, everyone seems to be hiding something: the unexplained departure of Dr. Sheehan, Rachel's psychiatrist, the day after she vanished, patient 67…

As he investigates, Teddy finds out that the asylum may be running illegal experiments, in the line of the horrors he saw when liberating the concentration camps. It also reopens a more recent wound: the death of his wife in a fire set by a madman… who is said to be in Ward C, home to the island's most dangerous patients.

Realizing he has fallen into a trap and may never be allowed to leave, he tries to escape, but ends up searching the lighthouse to free his partner, who has mysteriously disappeared. The lighthouse is supposedly where the most atrocious experiments on Shutter Island take place.

### Spoiler

In the lighthouse, Teddy Daniels finds only the asylum's chief psychiatrist, Dr. John Cawley. Cawley explains that he is in fact one of the Ward C patients (number 67), brought here after one tragedy too many. His wife was manic-depressive, and he ignored the doctors' warnings. One day, coming home from a case that kept him away for several days, he found she had drowned their three children. He decided to kill her to "set her free", but his mind refused that reality and made up a whole story so he could forget the trauma. Dr. Cawley explains that, with Dr. Sheehan (actually Chuck Aule, his partner), they staged this life-size "role-play" to confront him with reality and pull him out of his denial.

## Characters

- **Marshal Teddy Daniels**: a decorated veteran who took part in liberating the concentration camps during the war. His wife died a few years ago, and he can't get over it.
- **Marshal Chuck Aule**: Teddy's new partner. We know little about his past, except that he has just transferred from the West Coast. He suddenly disappears on the island.
- **Dr. John Cawley**: the asylum's chief psychiatrist. He claims a modern approach to psychiatry and says he wants to put an end to the barbaric methods of the past (trepanation and the like).
- **Dolores Chanal**: Teddy's late wife. She sometimes seems to speak to him inside his head.

## Analysis

I had already seen Scorsese's film twice a few years ago, and it had blown my mind. I wondered how the book could pull off the same feat, and I wasn't disappointed! First, it scatters at least as many clues about the ending as the film does. Then, Dennis Lehane puts us so completely inside Teddy's head that, even knowing how the film ends, I caught myself thinking "the book must have a different ending". The passage describing how Teddy's wife really died is incredibly moving; it brought tears to my eyes.

## What works

- **The setting.** A true locked-room story: a prison island in the middle of a storm, the perfect atmosphere for a thriller.
- **The psychology.** When everything seems to be against you, should you question yourself or fight the whole system?
- **The tragedy.** Or rather the tragedies Teddy goes through: how much can a man bear before sliding into madness?

## What is harder to take

- **The style.** I don't read many thrillers, so I can't tell whether it comes with the genre, but I found that the story took precedence over the style, and that the author "settles" for telling it, nothing more.
- **The ending.** I don't mean the twist, but the last pages, which say too much about Teddy's answer to the question "Is it better to live as a monster or die as a good man?". I would have liked readers to answer it on their own.

## Verdict

To date, the best thriller I've read. I was completely swept away by the story, the atmosphere and the character of Teddy Daniels. Knowing the ending doesn't spoil the read in the slightest; I'd even say it makes you pay closer attention to the clues Lehane leaves along the way, which makes it all the richer.
